Abstract—Automatic modulation recognition (AMR) is crucial
for efficient modulation type recognition in modern wireless sys-
tems, but its performance suffers from low signal-to-noise ratios
(SNR). This paper proposes an adaptive denoising automatic
modulation recognition network (AD-AMR Net) to improve its
performance, which incorporates an adaptive denoising module
(ADM) to alleviate noise and a feature extraction module (FEM)
to extract multi-scale features from the denoised signals. Com-
pared to conventional AMR schemes, AD-AMR Net demonstrates
better recognition accuracy under low SNRs, achieving 84.8%
average accuracy in the 0 - 10 dB range versus 80 - 82%
for conventional methods. Overall, the proposed model achieves
64.6% accuracy on the RadioML dataset, substantially outper-
forming previous deep learning techniques. Key innovations of
adaptive denoising approach and multi-scale feature extraction
enable major performance gains in low SNR scenarios.
Index Terms—Automatic modulation recognition, deep learn-
ing, efficient channel attention, adaptive denoising.
I. INTRODUCTION
Automatic modulation recognition (AMR) is crucial for
modern wireless communication systems to efficiently clas-
sify modulation types, laying the groundwork for subsequent
signal processing and enhancing communication security and
efficiency. AMR’s role extends beyond spectrum management;
it is pivotal in distinguishing between jamming noise and
legitimate signals, thereby ensuring the reliability and integrity
of communications in scenarios susceptible to interference and
malicious disruptions [2]. This capability is increasingly vital
in complex wireless environments characterized by pervasive
noise, interference, and distortion, where traditional AMR
approaches often falter [3].
Generally, AMR can be addressed as a pattern recognition
task. Traditional techniques used for AMR tasks can be divided
into two categories: likelihood-based methods [4] and feature-
based methods [5]. Likelihood-based methods treat AMR as a
multiple-hypothesis testing problem [6], make assumptions for
different modulation schemes, and compute the likelihood ratio
of received signals under different hypotheses. The modulation

type of the received signals is then determined by comparing
the likelihood ratio with a predefined threshold. Although
likelihood-based methods are optimal in the Bayesian sense,
they often require high computational complexity and prior
knowledge for modulation recognition, which would be sensi-
tive to unknown channel conditions [7]. Feature-based methods
have been shown to give sub-optimal solutions but are easier to
be implemented and have lower computational complexity [8],
in which the modulation type is determined using relevant
features from the received signals.
Recently, Deep Learning (DL) has been employed in AMR
to autonomously extract complex features directly from raw
signals. O’Shea et al. [9] proposed a convolutional neu-
ral network (CNN) for AMR. Their work originated DL
in AMR and achieved better performance than traditional
methods that use hand-crafted features. Though pioneering,
their model does not consider real environment interfer-
ences. Sainath et al. [10] combined convolutional layers,
long short-term memory (LSTM), and fully connected lay-
ers to extract spatial and temporal features from the input
signals. Unfortunately, LSTM struggles with long input se-
quences. Liu et al. [11] employs residual network (ResNet) for
AMR, which uses skip connection [12] to reduce the vanishing
gradient problem of deep neural networks. Nonetheless, it does
not address low SNR issues. Recent works have focused on
frequency domain features. Zhang et al. [13] introduced an
adaptive wavelet network (AWN) that uses adaptive wavelet
transforms to extract multi-level frequency features from sig-
nals. Zhang et al. [14] proposed a frequency learning attention
network that integrated squeeze and excitation (SE) blocks [15]
with ResNet to further improve the performance of AMR. On
this basis, Zhang et al. [16] proposed a network that uses
frequency domain features to improve performance under low
SNRs but still struggles with long input sequences of wideband
signals.
To address the above limitations, this paper proposes a novel
deep-learning framework for AMR, named adaptive denoising
automatic modulation recognition network (AD-AMR Net),
which significantly improves the performance of AMR under
low SNR conditions, and is time-efficient to deal with long
input sequence of wideband signals. The main contributions
of this paper are summarized as follows:

II. PROPOSED ADAPTIVE DENOISING AUTOMATIC
MODULATION RECOGNITION NETWORK
A. Model Architecture
Fig. 1 depicts the overall architecture of the proposed AD-
AMR Net. The input signal x ∈R2×L is first input into
the ADM to automatically extract pertinent information and
lower the noise level, in which we also integrated ECA to
improve its generalization ability. The output of the ADM is
then fed into the FEM that is built by stacking multiple ERSBs
to extract multi-scale features from the denoised signals. The
ERSB is an improved version of RSB that also utilizes ECA
to improve performance and reduce computational complexity.
Finally, the extracted features are flattened and put into a Fully
Connected Layer (FCL) followed by a softmax layer to obtain
the predicted probability distribution of modulation types.
B. Adaptive Denoising Module
To address noise and offset in complex time series, we
integrated ECA with WaveUNet to create the ADM. This
design enhances signal clarity by selectively suppressing noise
while maintaining the original signal’s integrity. Empirical
evidence supports the efficacy of this technique, showing it
produces a denoised signal version, effectively isolating clean
components from noise and offsets. [21]. By passing noisy
signals through the ADM, we can disentangle the clean signal
components from the noise and offsets.
The ADM architecture, depicted in the left part of Fig. 2,
employs a sequence of downsample and upsample blocks to
enhance signal quality by integrating key information and
reducing noise. Initially, the signal undergoes L downsample
blocks, each comprising a 1D convolutional layer with a kernel
size of 15, followed by downsampling and an Efficient Channel
Attention (ECA) module. This process condenses information,
focusing on broader signal patterns and diminishing fine-
grained noise. Subsequently, L upsample blocks, each featur-
ing a 1D convolutional layer (kernel size 5), upsampling, and
an ECA, refine the resolution, re-incorporating details from
the broader context. ECAs within these blocks are designed
to emphasize salient features. Skip connections facilitate the
merging of local and global features, ensuring comprehensive
context integration. A concluding 1D convolutional layer (ker-
nel size 1) synthesizes the denoised output by consolidating
channel-wise information, allowing the ADM to effectively
distinguish between noise and relevant signal components
through multi-scale processing.
C. Feature Extraction Module
FEM is designed to extract multi-scale features from the de-
noised signals. The FEM is built by stacking multiple ERSBs
in a hierarchical manner adopted from the ResNet architecture.
Based on the RSB of Deep Residual Shrinkage Network [19],
ERSB replaces the original SE block with a more efficient
ECA module. As shown in the top right of Fig. 2. ERSB can
be broken down into two convolutional layers with an identity
shortcut, an adaptive soft-thresholding operation, and an ECA
module. The identity shortcuts allow unimpeded information
flow and gradient propagation through the network during

D. Efficient Channel Attention
Channel attention mechanisms are widely used in today’s
CNNs for various tasks. The SE block is a popular channel at-
tention mechanism that uses a squeeze and excitation operation
to capture channel-wise dependencies. However, the SE block
relies on a computationally expensive fully connected layer
to learn attention weights, which may lead to overfitting. To
address this issue, we introduce the ECA module, a lightweight
yet effective channel attention mechanism designed specifi-
cally for high generalization ability. As shown in the bottom
right of Fig. 2, the ECA module consists of a global average
pooling (GAP) layer, a 1D convolutional layer, and a sigmoid
activation, therefore requiring far fewer parameters than the
SE block. The GAP layer summarizes feature maps into a
global context vector that captures cross-channel dependen-
cies. The 1D convolutional layer then transforms this context
into channel-wise attention weights, which are scaled between
0 and 1 by the sigmoid function, which is defined as:
sigmoid(x) =

By performing element-wise multiplication with the input fea-
tures, the ECA highlights informative features and suppresses
less relevant ones. Despite its simplicity, empirical results show
that the ECA module can achieve better performance than the
SE block while using fewer parameters and less computation.

B. Baseline and Metrics
For evaluation, the following methods will be compared
with the proposed scheme as benchmarks:
1) CNN [9]: CNN is a vanilla convolutional neural network
that uses convolutional layers to extract features from
complex signal inputs and classify modulation types.
2) CLDNN [10]: CLDNN combines CNN and RNN ar-
chitectures, using four convolutional layers, one LSTM
layer, and two fully connected layers.
3) ResNet [11]: ResNet is a deep convolution network
architecture that uses skip connection to reduce the
vanishing gradient problem of deep neural networks.
4) SENet [14]: SENet integrated squeeze and excitation
(SE) blocks with ResNet to improve feature learning.
5) AWN [13]: Adaptive Wavelet Network (AWN) utilizes
wavelet transforms to extract frequency information.
We use the overall accuracy (OA), macro-averaged F1-score,
Kappa coefficient, and number of parameters as the evaluation
metrics.

E. Discussions
Since the ADM can be used as a standalone module
that can be integrated into various existing models, we also
conducted experiments to assess the plug-and-play ability of
the ADM by integrating it into other models. The experimental
configurations are as follows:
1) ADM-CNN: CNN with ADM.
2) ADM-CLDNN: CLDNN with ADM.
3) ADM-AWN: AWN with ADM.
As outlined in Table V. The results validate the efficacy
of the ADM for its plug-and-play ability. Its noise reduction
capabilities translate into consistent accuracy improvements in
both high and low SNR settings, which shows that the module
has broad applicability.
IV. CONCLUSION
Through adaptive denoising and multi-scale feature ex-
traction, the proposed AD-AMR Net can address the major
challenges of performance degradation in low SNR conditions.
Numerical experiments validate AD-AMR Net’s performance
in low SNR scenarios. The adaptive denoising capability en-
ables accurate classification under noisy conditions by filtering
out distortions. Ablation studies verify the function of key
components of ADM and FEM. Integrating the ADM into
other architectures like CNN, CLDNN and AWN yields overall
accuracy improvements up to 3.37% and average gains of
6.67% from 0 - 10 dB SNR. This highlights the versatility of
ADM in improving performance under low SNRs. By tackling
the pressing challenges of noise and low SNR robustness,
the proposed AD-AMR Net architecture achieves better per-
formance on the RadioML 2018.01A dataset compared with
existing schemes.
